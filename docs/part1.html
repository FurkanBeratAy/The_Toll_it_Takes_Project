Did EJ-designated sites fare worse?

EJ status is confounded with zone status in this data: every in-zone monitor except Queensboro sits in a designated tract, and so does every outside-zone monitor with a full pre-toll record. Panel estimates (site + date fixed effects, 11 sites) of an EJ gap run from −0.38 to +0.02 µg/m³ depending on flag definition, none significant (p = 0.65–0.96). What the data shows is geography: inside the zone improved; no outside-zone site did — EJ or not, Bronx or not (see Check 9).

Nine independent tests of the traffic-diversion hypothesis. The main ITS estimates are underpowered (Mott Haven p = 0.61; five ITS sites, eleven in the panel) and PM2.5 is driven largely by regional sources. 2. Peak-vs-overnight PM2.5 difference

The toll is 75% cheaper overnight. If diversion is toll-driven, peak-hour PM2.5 should rise relative to overnight. Daily outcome: mean peak PM2.5 minus mean overnight PM2.5 (≥8 peak hours, ≥5 overnight hours required). QC and VW differences used as controls.

How to read this: Each bar is how much the daytime-minus-nighttime gap changed after the toll. Smoke and weather affect both and cancel; extra daytime traffic does not. Dark bar = all days; light bar = weekdays only.

How to read this: Each bar is how much the daytime-minus-nighttime gap changed after the toll. Smoke and weather affect both and cancel; extra daytime traffic does not. Dark bar = all days; light bar = weekdays only. Black outline = 95% CI excludes zero (weekdays only). Faded bars = sites with fewer than 200 pre-toll days (Hamilton Bridge, SI Expwy, BQE).

At Mott Haven, peak-hour PM2.5 was 0.219 µg/m³ higher than overnight after the toll (p=0.419). The weekday-only version gives β=+0.058 (p=0.851). Cross Bronx Expy is the one site where this test is informative: all-days β=+0.60 (p=0.089), weekday-only β=+0.90 (p=0.039) — the only significant result among the 16 peak/overnight tests at the 8 sites with ≥200 pre-toll days. Hamilton Bridge, SI Expwy, and BQE are shown faded: all three have fewer than 200 pre-toll days in the Van Wyck window (short baseline, not non-significance). Regional weather differences cancel in the differenced series, so the Cross Bronx result is harder to attribute to non-traffic sources.

3. Placebo-in-time

Using pre-toll data only, a fake intervention is placed on the 1st of each month from July 2021 through June 2024 (up to 36 placebos). The long-baseline QC-only spec is used for all sites. Each placebo requires ≥90 observed days on each side and ≥60% data coverage in the surrounding 6-month windows. Empirical p = share of |β_placebo| ≥ |β_observed|.

How to read this: Grey bars = estimates from fake toll dates; red line = estimate with the real date. A red line inside a very wide pile means the method can't distinguish real from fake.

Distribution of placebo β_post by site (red line = observed long-baseline β)

How to read this: Grey bars = estimates from fake toll dates; red line = estimate with the real date. A red line inside a very wide pile means the method can't distinguish real from fake.

The placebo test is inconclusive for this dataset. The long-baseline QC-only specification is highly unstable: placebo β distributions have SD of 7–17 µg/m³ across the five sites (Queensboro placebo mean +15.7 µg/m³; Williamsburg has only 3 valid placebos), driven by large data gaps (268–791 days), the COVID period, and wildfire months. Observed effects of 1–3 µg/m³ are not exceptional against a baseline that swings ±15 µg/m³. The placebo test cannot distinguish signal from instability, so empirical p-values are not reported. The long-baseline column in the ITS table should be read as a sensitivity note, not a primary result.

5. Mechanism check

In the pre-toll period (2022–2024), Mott Haven daily PM2.5 is regressed on Throgs Neck Bridge daily crossings (per 1,000 vehicles), Queens College, seasonal terms, day-of-week dummies, and weather covariates (HAC SE, maxlags=30). The estimated slope is multiplied by the post-toll Throgs Neck residual (+2,041 veh/day) to produce an implied PM2.5 change, then compared to the observed ITS step-change.

Mott Haven PM2.5 vs Throgs Neck volume (pre-toll, regression line shown)

How to read this: Each dot is one pre-toll day. A steep line would mean bridge traffic drives Mott Haven air. The line is nearly flat.

In the pre-toll period (2022–2024), each additional 1,000 vehicles/day at Throgs Neck Bridge is associated with β=+0.0181 µg/m³ at Mott Haven (p=0.272, HAC SE, n=144). Multiplying by the post-toll Throgs Neck residual of +2041 veh/day implies +0.04 µg/m³ — about one-eighth of the observed ITS step-change of +0.30 µg/m³, so traffic diversion via Throgs Neck cannot account for most of the Mott Haven increase. However, consistency of magnitudes alone does not establish causation; unmeasured confounders could produce the same pattern.

6. Bronx NO2 (EPA AQS hourly, 2022–2025)

Hourly NO2 from two EPA AQS SLAMS monitors in the Bronx — IS 52 (681 Kelly St, South Bronx; Site 110) and Pfizer Lab Site (Southern Blvd & 200th St, North Bronx; Site 133) — aggregated to daily means and compared before and after the toll. Background control: daily mean of Queens EPA sites 124 and 125. Longer baseline ITS uses 2022-01-01 start with HAC SE (maxlags=30). Note: neither monitor is on the Bruckner Expressway or the Major Deegan corridor.

Raw 2024-vs-2025 calendar-year comparison: IS 52 fell −0.43 ppb (from 14.70 to 14.27); Pfizer Lab fell −0.38 ppb (from 12.48 to 12.10). The Queens control sites changed +0.21 ppb (Site 124) and −0.68 ppb (Site 125) over the same period — changes of comparable magnitude and direction. The longer-baseline ITS (2022-01-01 to present) gives β = −5.99 ppb at IS 52 (p = 0.079, n_pre = 1,089) and β = −3.68 ppb at Pfizer Lab (p = 0.240, n_pre = 1,097), neither significant at α = 0.05. Placebo tests (pre-toll data only, 24 placebo dates July 2022 – June 2024) give empirical p = 0.29 for IS 52 and 0.50 for Pfizer Lab, meaning betas this large arise routinely from seasonal variation in the pre-toll record. No toll effect on Bronx NO2 is detectable.

How this compares to the South Bronx Unite study

The South Bronx Unite community network documented PM₂.₅ rising at 12–14 of 19 sensors between January 2024 and December 2025, averaging approximately +0.22 µg/m³ and reaching +1.29 µg/m³ near expressways (NYC Congestion Pricing Datathon brief). Our Check 7 matched-day raw changes at the two Bronx NYCCAS sites fall within the range South Bronx Unite reports: Mott Haven +0.08 µg/m³, Cross Bronx −0.16 µg/m³ — small and mixed in sign (2025 vs. 2024).

The difference between the two studies is the counterfactual. South Bronx Unite reports raw year-over-year change. When we subtract a control monitor, the same upward shift appears at every outside-zone site regardless of borough or EJ status. In 2025, the three primary-set PurpleAir sensors each rose relative to Van Wyck: 89th & Ridge Ave in Bay Ridge +0.85 µg/m³, RGBIV in Brooklyn Heights +1.26 µg/m³, FA_O5 in East Harlem +1.19 µg/m³ (Check 9). Their raw year-over-year changes (+0.25, +0.48, and +0.74 µg/m³) fall within the range South Bronx Unite reports across its network.

On the expressway question, our one expressway-adjacent peak-hour result points the same way. Cross Bronx Expressway, weekday peak hours (Check 2): βpost = +0.90 µg/m³ (95% CI +0.04 to +1.75, p = 0.04) — values that fall within the range South Bronx Unite reports across its network. That is one significant result among the peak-hour tests — a hint, not a finding.

The two studies see the same underlying data. PM₂.₅ did not fall at outside-zone Bronx sites. The disagreement is about what to compare it to. Relative to the regional background (the control monitors), the Bronx moved the same way as Bay Ridge, Brooklyn Heights, and East Harlem. The organizing variable is inside versus outside the toll zone — not EJ status, not the Bronx.

What if the expressways were boulevards?

The NYCCAS monitor at Mott Haven sits 44 m from the Major Deegan Expressway and 633 m from the Bruckner Expressway. The Major Deegan carries roughly 100,000–130,000 vehicles per day — approximately 7,000–8,500 vehicles per hour bidirectional at peak (Bronx MIS traffic counts). The congestion toll has little leverage over an expressway that mostly bypasses the zone.

To translate that volume into a rough PM₂.₅ figure, two sanity checks converge. Arithmetically, the pre-toll mechanism coefficient (β = 0.018 µg/m³ per 1,000 veh/day at Throgs Neck, Check 5, p = 0.27) times 100,000–130,000 veh/day gives 1.8–2.4 µg/m³ — an upper bound, because the coefficient comes from a single bridge, not the full local source mix. Independently, the NYC Health Department's congestion pricing air quality report (August 2026) estimates that vehicle traffic contributes about 14% of the city's PM₂.₅ emissions. Applied to Mott Haven's pre-toll mean of 7.45 µg/m³, that implies roughly 1.04 µg/m³ from all traffic combined. Both approaches point to a defensible range of about 1–2 µg/m³ attributable to traffic at this site.

Now the boulevard scenario. If the Major Deegan were redesigned to carry half its current volume — say, 50,000–65,000 vehicles per day — the implied PM₂.₅ reduction at the monitor would be on the order of 0.5–1 µg/m³. That is larger than any change measured outside the zone in this report, and comparable to what the toll delivered inside it. And it would be delivered directly to the nine schools within 400 m of the monitor.

This is back-of-envelope, not a model. It treats the mechanism coefficient as causal, ignores local mixing and fleet composition, and the Health Department's 14% figure is an emissions share for the whole city applied to a measured concentration at a single site. But the order of magnitude is the point: street-network restructuring 44 m from the monitor may have more leverage on Mott Haven air quality than a toll that reduces vehicles entering a zone roughly 6 km to the south.
